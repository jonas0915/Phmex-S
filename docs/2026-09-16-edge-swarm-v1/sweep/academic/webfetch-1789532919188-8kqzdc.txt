We assess the information content of order flow in the emerging cryptocurrency mar-
ket. Our analysis is based on a large cross-section of cryptocurrencies and a set of
international order flows denominated in 11 major currencies that reflect world order
flow. We find that world order flow has strong explanatory power for contempora-
neous cryptocurrency returns. Using machine learning techniques for out-of-sample
prediction, we also find that order flow has high predictive ability for daily cryp-
tocurrency returns. For example, long-short portfolios sorted on machine learning
forecasts conditioning on daily order flow exhibit an alpha of up to 0 .79% per day
with an annualized Sharpe ratio of 3 .63. Order flow tends to dominate economic
fundamentals for prediction, especially in the context of non-linear machine learning
models, and its performance cannot be explained by economic restrictions such as
short-sale constraints, transaction costs or limits to arbitrage. Overall, we provide
comprehensive evidence indicating order flow has a permanent effect for cryptocur-
rency returns.

1.1 Introduction
The emergence of the cryptocurrency market over the past decade has been accompa-
nied by remarkable growth. Market capitalization has increased from $11 billion US
dollars (USD) in 2014 to a peak of $2.9 trillion USD in 2021. Similarly, daily trading
volume has risen from $40 million USD in 2014 to a peak of $308 billion USD in
2021. Unlike traditional foreign exchange markets, where trading volume is concen-
trated among a few major dealers (Menkhoff et al., 2016), cryptocurrency markets
consist of independently owned, non-integrated exchanges that operate in parallel
across countries and multiple currencies (Makarov and Schoar, 2020). Furthermore,
1
cryptocurrency markets are relatively less liquid and face more pervasive asymmetric
information compared to traditional markets (Bianchi, Babiak and Dickerson, 2022).
These unique features make cryptocurrency markets a novel setting for studying the
role of order flow in the price formation process. 1
In this context, this paper contributes to the emerging cryptocurrency literature by
assessing the information content of order flow for explaining and predicting cryp-
tocurrency returns. Specifically, we address three related questions. First, can order
flow explain the cross-section of cryptocurrency returns? Second, does order flow con-
vey predictive information for the cross-section of cryptocurrency returns? Lastly,
is there valuable information contained in international order flows that can not be
explained by US order flow? Addressing these questions is relevant to the study of
market microstructure and paves the way for a deeper investigation into the eco-
nomic mechanisms driving order flow and price discovery in cryptocurrency markets.
Although for traditional financial markets these questions have been addressed by
a long line of research, for cryptocurrencies they remain open. Accordingly, by fo-
cusing on the information conveyed by order flow in the cryptocurrency market, this
paper bridges a gap in the literature.
There are several studies related to the theory and empirical evidence on the effect
of order flow on fiat exchange rates. For example Evans and Lyons (2002, 2005) pro-

Order flow is a measure of the net demand for a particular cryptocurrency defined
in this study as the log difference between buyer-initiated and seller-initiated trans-
action volume denominated in a particular fiat currency over a period of time. In
general, there are three economic views on the extent to which order flow may be
related to cryptocurrency returns. First, it is possible that order flow is in fact unre-
lated to cryptocurrency returns. In this case, it is economic fundamentals, not order
flow, that may explain and predict cryptocurrency returns. We refer to this view
as the “no-predictability view” and it is essentially the null hypothesis tested in our

Second, it is possible that order flow has a transitory effect on cryptocurrency returns.
In this case, order flow has a temporary but not a permanent effect. The market
microstructure literature associates transitory effects with changes in liquidity, price
pressure and temporary preference shocks (see, e.g., Froot and Ramadorai, 2005, and
Menkhoff et al., 2016). We refer to this view as the “transitory view.”
Finally, third, it is possible that order flow has a permanent effect on cryptocur-
rency returns. The market microstructure literature associates permanent effects
with asymmetric information among market participants so that trades convey in-
formation with a persistent effect on security prices (see, e.g., Glosten and Milgrom,
1985, Kyle, 1985, and Hasbrouck, 1988, 1991). According to this view, if trades con-
vey information about future economic fundamentals that is not currently known by
all market participants, then order flow acts as the key vehicle that impounds this
information into cryptocurrency returns through the process of price discovery (see,
e.g., Evans and Lyons, 2002, 2005, and Menkhoff et al., 2016). We refer to this view
as the “permanent view.”
Our empirical analysis employs daily data from a cross-section of 84 cryptocurrencies
for the sample period of January 1, 2018 to June 30, 2022. We use a rich dataset
of order flows denominated in 11 fiat currencies, including the G10 currencies plus
South Korea, which is a prominent center for cryptocurrency trading. We then
form a measure of world order flow by aggregating the 11 international order flows.
Accordingly, our analysis assesses the information content of world order flow and
4
disaggregated international order flows, including US order flow. In what follows, we
discuss our empirical approach and main findings step by step.
We begin by estimating panel regressions to assess whether order flow can explain
and predict cryptocurrency returns in sample. The aim of this analysis is not to
prove causality but to examine the explanatory and predictive power of order flow
for returns. The panel regressions condition on combinations of world order flow and
the 11 international order flows together with a set of coin-specific control variables
and economic fundamentals. The economic fundamentals included are based on
what has been widely used in the literature (see for e.g.Welch and Goyal, 2008). We
estimate contemporaneous and predictive panel regressions at the daily and weekly
frequency.
With respect to the in-sample evidence, our main finding is that world order flow
has significant explanatory and predictive power for the cross-section of cryptocur-
rency returns. The contemporaneous relation of world order flow and cryptocurrency
returns is positive and highly significant. World order flow together with control vari-
ables can explain about 10% of daily returns and 20% of weekly returns.
For predictive regressions, it is crucial to separate two opposing components of order
flow: a transitory component that reverses in the short term and a permanent com-
ponent that persists over the long term. Following Bianchi, Babiak and Dickerson
(2022), we use lagged returns as a proxy for short-term reversal. In other words, the
predictive regressions condition on lagged order flow, while controlling for lagged re-
turns. We find that the portion of lagged order flow that is uncorrelated with lagged
5
returns has a positive and significant predictive relation with future returns. The
positive coefficient of world order flow for both daily and weekly returns provides
empirical support for the permanent view.
In considering all international order flows, world order flow consistently exhibits
the highest and most significant coefficient compared to all international order flows.
In contemporaneous regressions, the US order flow is also significant. In predictive
regressions, however, the significance of the US order flow disappears in the presence
of world order flow. Overall, these findings indicate that world order flow is a more
powerful and significant predictor than any of the individual international order flows
in the context of in-sample panel regressions.
In summary, our analysis provides compelling empirical evidence to establish that
order flow has strong explanatory and predictive power in an in-sample setting. Our
findings indicate that order flow has a permanent effect for cryptocurrency returns
since this effect is consistently positive and stronger for weekly compared to daily
returns.
Our empirical analysis is related to the study of Makarov and Schoar (2020), who
examine the relation between bitcoin returns and order flow. However, the focus
of Makarov and Schoar (2020) is on assessing arbitrage opportunities across cryp-
tocurrency exchanges. Therefore, it substantially differs from our study since it only
focuses on one coin. In general, our approach is distinct from the existing literature
as indicated by the use of a large cross-section of coins and a large cross-section of
order flows denominated in different fiat currencies.

1.2 Data
1.2.1 Cryptocurrency Returns
Our empirical analysis employs data from a cross-section of 84 cryptocurrencies for
the sample period of January 1, 2018 to June 30, 2022. For each cryptocurrency
(crypto or coin), we collect daily US dollar (USD) prices from coinmarketcap.com
(henceforth CMC) at 00:00am GMT time. The CMC data aggregates prices from
over 700 exchanges and is a reliable source of data that is used extensively in the
cryptocurrency literature (for a detailed description of the CMC data, see, e.g., Liu
and Tsyvinski, 2021 and Liu, Tsyvinski and Wu, 2022). We exclude weekends and
US holidays from the daily sample.
The CMC database contains data on thousands of coins but most have low market
capitalization and/or limited historical data. To obtain the final cross-section of
84 coins, we impose three criteria to ensure the liquidity of our sample. First, we
require that coins must have a market capitalization of greater than 1 million USD on
the first day of the sample period. Second, coins must be continuously traded with

1.2.3 Control Variables
We use a combination of coin-specific variables and economic fundamentals as con-
trols in the empirical analysis. The coin-specific variables include market capitaliza-
tion (henceforth market cap), total volume and volatility. Total volume is the sum
of a coin’s 24-hour trading volume across all exchanges. Volume and market cap are
expressed in logs. Volatility is defined as the log difference between a coin’s high
price and low price on a given day. As mentioned earlier, all coin-specific data other
than order flow are obtained from CMC.
The economic fundamentals used in our analysis include the following. The short
rate is defined as the 3-month US T-bill rate. The term spread is defined as the
difference between the 10-year and 3-month US treasury rates. The default spread is
defined as the difference between the BAA- and AAA-rated corporate bond yields.
All interest rate data are obtained from the Federal Reserve Bank of St. Louis. The
TED spread is defined as the difference between the 3-month T-Bill rate and the 3-
month LIBOR rate, which is obtained from the LSEG Eikon database. The S&P 500
index returns (including dividends) are from the CRSP database. The MSCI global
index returns are taken from msci.com. The VIX index is obtained from CBOE.com.

1.3 In-Sample Analysis
Our empirical approach is based on balanced panel regressions. This section presents
the in-sample panel regression results on the effect of order flow in explaining and
predicting cryptocurrency returns. We begin with explanatory and predictive re-
gressions that include world order flow followed by regressions with all international
order flows. Finally, we briefly discuss the results of the panel regressions which in-
clude US order flow. We note that the objective of this study is not to develop fully
specified explanatory and predictive models of cryptocurrency returns, but rather to
assess whether order flow exhibits statistically significant explanatory and predictive
power. Accordingly, while potential endogeneity and dynamic panel bias are ac-
12
knowledged, advanced estimators such as GMM or instrumental variable approaches
are not employed, as the focus lies on inference regarding the marginal effect of or-
der flow within a consistent fixed-effects framework. To address the possibility of
reverse causality, a complementary vector autoregression (V AR) framework is later
employed in chapter 3 to examine the potential bidirectional relationship between
order flow and returns.
1.3.1 Panel Regressions with World Order Flow
Contemporaneous Regressions
Can world order flow explain cryptocurrency returns? We address this question by
estimating panel regressions that condition on contemporaneous world order flow
and the control variables. The panel regressions are estimated using the full sample
ranging from February 14, 2018 to June 30, 2022, and include coin fixed effects. The
t-statistics shown in all panel regressions are computed using standard errors which
are clustered by time and by coin. The results for both daily and weekly data are
reported in Table 1.5. 5
Our main empirical finding is that world order flow has a positive contemporaneous
relation to returns, which is highly significant in all cases. Therefore, world order
flow does explain cryptocurrency returns. For example, in the presence of control
variables, a one standard deviation increase in world order flow is associated with a

2.2% increase in returns at the daily frequency and a 4 .0% increase at the weekly

and Schoar (2020), who find a positive contemporaneous relation between order flow

regressions and 19.6% for weekly regressions. A possible explanation for the stronger
weekly results is that order flow at the daily horizon may include uninformed traders
(also referred to as liquidity or noise traders). Since the trades of uninformed traders
are driven primarily by liquidity preferences than information, the effect of these
traders on the price tends to be transitory. Aggregating order flow over one week
mitigates the effect of market microstructure noise. Consequently, order flow may
explain a greater variation of price movements at the weekly than the daily horizon.
This is in line with a long literature on market microstructure, which distinguishes
between informed and uninformed traders and assesses the effect of information-
based trading on asset prices. See, for example, Glosten and Milgrom (1985), Easley
and O’Hara (1987), and Easley et al. (1996).
In addition to order flow, several control variables are also significant. For daily
regressions, volume and volatility are significant. For weekly regressions, market
cap, volume, volatility, the S&P 500 return, the MSCI global index return and the
14
term spread are all significant. In conclusion, world order flow has strong and highly
significant explanatory power for the cross-section of cryptocurrency returns in the
presence of crypto-specific control variables and standard economic fundamentals.
Predictive Regressions
Can world order flow predict cryptocurrency returns? We address this question by
estimating the same panel regressions as previously but with lagged values for all
independent variables. Specifically, we condition on the same control variables plus
lagged cryptocurrency returns. Short-term returns exhibit a negative autocorrelation
due to transitory effects such as price pressure and other short-term liquidity effects,
resulting in reversal, i.e., a negative relation between lagged and future returns.
Table 1.5 shows that lagged order flow is positively (contemporaneously) related to
lagged returns, suggesting that order flow has two opposing components: a transitory
component that reverses in the short term and a permanent component that persists
in the long term. Following Bianchi, Babiak and Dickerson (2022), we use lagged
returns as a proxy for short-term liquidity effects, and assess the predictive relation
of lagged order flow with future returns, while controlling for lagged returns.
The results reported in Table 1.6 indicate that world order flow is a highly significant
predictor of both one-day ahead and one-week ahead cryptocurrency returns. The
daily predictive regressions exhibit an interesting pattern. Lagged order flow on its
own is not significant (t-stat=0.62). However, after controlling for lagged returns,
it is highly significant (t-stat=2.58). In contrast, lagged returns have a negative
15
and significant effect (t-stat=-3.25). These findings confirm that lagged order flow
has two opposing components: the transitory component correlated with lagged
returns that exhibits short-term reversal, and a permanent component, uncorrelated
to lagged returns, that is positively related to future returns. By conditioning on
both lagged order flow and lagged returns, we uncover the positive predictive ability
of daily world order flow.
For the weekly predictive regressions, world order flow has a consistent positive
and significant predictive power for future returns whether we condition on lagged
returns or not. Weekly lagged returns still exhibit a negative coefficient but it is
not significant. This aligns with transitory effects exerting a stronger impact on
prices at the daily horizon, while at the weekly horizon, permanent effects such as
adverse selection become more dominant. Accordingly, the component of lagged
weekly order flow that is uncorrelated with lagged weekly returns appears to be
the dominant component of weekly order flow leading to consistently positive and
significant predictions.
The strong positive predictive effect of world order flow on both daily and weekly
returns is consistent with the permanent view. Our findings provide evidence against
both the non-predictability and the transitory view. Specifically, in the full speci-
fication that includes the control variables, an increase in lagged world order flow
by one standard deviation is associated with a 0.2% increase in daily returns and a
0.9% increase in weekly returns. The increasing strength of this predictive relation
for longer horizons provides empirical support for the permanent view of the effect
of order flow on cryptocurrency returns.
16
Relevant to our analysis, Makarov and Schoar (2020) study the relation between
order flow and future returns specifically for bitcoin. They find a significant negative
relation between order flow and one-day-ahead returns, and a positive but statisti-
cally insignificant relation with one-week-ahead returns. In this context, by using
a broad cross-section of cryptocurrencies, our approach reveals the full predictive
power of order flow.
In addition to order flow and lagged returns, several control variables are significant
in the predictive regressions. For the daily predictive regressions, the market cap,
volatility and the short rate are significant. For the weekly predictive regressions,
the market cap, volume, volatility and the MSCI global index return are significant.
Finally, note that the daily predictive regression delivers an ¯R2 = 1 .2% and the
weekly predictive regressions an ¯R2 = 3.3%.

In addition to panel regressions conditioning on just world order flow, we also esti-

regressions are set up in the same way as previously and condition on the same con-
trol variables. We estimate two types regressions: one with just the 11 international
order flows and one with the 11 order flows plus the aggregate world order flow.
We use daily and weekly data and repeat for both contemporaneous and predictive

Contemporaneous Regressions
The results from contemporaneous regressions are shown in Table 1.7. Our main
finding is that, for both daily and weekly returns, world order flow has the highest
and most significant coefficient. Specifically, a one standard deviation increase in
world order flow is associated with a 1.9% increase in returns at the daily horizon
(t-stat=11.03) and a 2.9% increase at the weekly horizon (t-stat=7.01). Other than
world order flow, US and Korean order flows remain significant in all specifications.
The full set of order flows together with the control variables explain 11.4% of daily
crypto returns and 20.3% of weekly returns.
These results justify the use of disaggregated international order flows (including US
order flow) over and above world order flow. They also motivate the inclusion of
South Korea to the sample since it is consistently significant. In short, the empirical
evidence indicates that order flows on a global scale have explanatory power for

1.4 Conclusion
The meteoric rise of cryptocurrencies as a new investment asset class has triggered
an emerging literature in financial economics. The foreign exchange literature has
established order flow as a prominent predictor of fiat currency returns representing
the microstructure approach to exchange rate prediction as an alternative to models
conditioning on standard economic fundamentals. For cryptocurrencies, however,
there is a gap in the literature as there is little work on the ability of order flow
to explain and predict cryptocurrency returns. Our analysis contributes to this
literature by highlighting the role of order flow for return prediction over and above
standard economic fundamentals.
We find that order flow matters both for explaining and for predicting the cross-
section of cryptocurrency returns. World order flow has a strong contemporaneous
and predictive relation to cryptocurrency returns in the context of in-sample panel
regressions. Specifically, the ¯R2 is equal to 10.4% for daily regressions and 19 .6% for
in what is known as the Kimchi premium (see, e.g., Makarov and Schoar, 2020).
21
weekly regressions. A one standard deviation increase in world order flow is associ-
ated with a 2 .2% increase in returns at the daily frequency and a 4 .0% increase at
the weekly frequency. Our predictive models show an increase in lagged world order
flow by one standard deviation is associated with a 0.2% increase in daily returns
and a 0.9% increase in weekly returns. The strong positive predictive effect of world
order flow on both daily and weekly returns is consistent with the permanent view.
In conclusion, our empirical evidence strongly indicates that information conveyed
by order flow matters for cryptocurrency returns.

2.1 Introduction
The rapid expansion of the cryptocurrency market over the past decade has caused
it to become the newest asset class to emerge onto the global finance stage. Its
unique characteristics such as being comprised of independently owned decentralized
exchanges, and greater opportunity for asymmetric information make it an inter-
esting environment to study the role of order flow in the price formation process
(Makarov and Schoar, 2020,Bianchi, Babiak and Dickerson, 2022). In the previous
36
chapter we established the strong explanatory and predictive significance of world
order flow on crypto returns in an in-sample setting. We also established that the
predictive information contained in order flow is permanent rather than transitory
in nature, as the positive significance of order flow for predicting returns became
stronger at longer horizons. While the in-sample significance is clear, the question of
whether this predictive significance holds when we move to an out-of-sample setting
remains unanswered. In this paper, we aim to address this gap in the literature and
deepen our understanding of how order flow impacts the price formation process in
the cryptocurrency market.
Specifically, we assess the out-of-sample predictive ability of order flow for daily
cryptocurrency returns using machine learning (ML) techniques. In recent literature,
machine learning has been used to forecast returns for the cross-section of stocks as
well as cryptocurrencies. For example, Gu, Kelly and Xiu (2020) employ various
linear and non-linear ML models to forecast a large cross-section of stocks with more
than 900 predictors. They find non-linear techniques are the top performing models,
providing significant gains to investors. Cakici et al. (2024) employ a variety of ML
models to predict a cross-section of over 500 cryptocurrency returns using a set of 41
characteristics. They find predictability is largely driven by a few characteristics but
ML models still provide a profitable strategy for investors. Similarly, Fieberg et al.
(2024) and Filippou, Rapach and Thimsen (2024) also use ML techniques to predict a
cross-section of cryptocurrency returns. Fieberg et al. (2024) use predictors derived
from technical signals and find these models outperform more widely used factor
models. Filippou, Rapach and Thimsen (2024) find predictors related to momentum,
37
size and value are important, and allowing for non-linear functional forms improves
performance. 1
ML methods are ideally suited for out-of-sample forecasting because they emphasize
techniques for variable selection and dimension reduction, which can accommodate
a large set of predictors as well as a richer specification of functional forms. For our
study we use the same data set from the in-sample analysis of chapter 1, comprised
of a cross-section of 84 coins spanning the sample period of January 1, 2018 to June
30, 2022. We employ a rich dataset of order flows denominated in 11 fiat currencies,
including the G10 currencies plus South Korea, which is a prominent center for
cryptocurrency trading. We then form a measure of world order flow by aggregating
the 11 international order flows. ML forecasting allows us to condition on the 11
international order flows and additional economic fundamentals for prediction of the
one-day ahead cryptocurrency returns.
This paper aims to address two important and related questions. First, do linear and
non-linear machine learning techniques improve the out-of-sample predictive power
of order flow relative to standard predictive approaches? And second, do models
that condition on our set of international order flows and aggregate world order flow
improve predictability over models that include economic fundamental predictors
only? We answer these questions in two main ways. First, we evaluate the statistical
performance of a variety of ML models conditioning on all 11 international order

Addressing these questions is relevant to the study of market microstructure and
deepens our understanding into the economic mechanisms driving order flow and
price discovery in cryptocurrency markets.
We estimate a set of linear and non-linear ML models, which include the follow-

component regression (PCR), random forest (RF), stochastic gradient boosted re-
gression trees (SGB), and neural networks with 1-4 hidden layers (NN1-NN4). In
addition, we compute forecast combinations across the linear models (L-Mean), and
the non-linear models (NL-Mean). 2

is the SGB model that conditions on all order flows with an out-of-sample R2 of
0.39%. In short, non-linear ML models conditioning on order flow have significant

strong performance, especially at the weekly frequency. For weekly portfolio sorts,
the long-short mean return spread is equal to 1 .83% per week, it is highly significant

on all order flows. Although all ML models perform well, the non-linear models con-

on order flow consistently outperform ML models conditioning on economic funda-
mentals. Non-linear ML models conditioning on order flow also outperform leading
ML-based benchmarks in the literature for forecasting cryptocurrency returns. For
example, the NL-Mean model in Filippou, Rapach and Thimsen (2024), which con-
ditions on 54 characteristics but excludes order flow, generates a Sharpe ratio of
2.57, while the same model conditioning on just order flow in this paper achieves
a Sharpe ratio of 3.61. Taken together, these findings indicate that there is signif-
icant economic value in conditioning on all international order flows in the context
of out-of-sample machine learning forecasts.
In summary, our analysis provides compelling empirical evidence to establish that
order flow has strong and economically valuable out-of-sample predictive power for
cryptocurrency returns. Our findings indicate that order flow has a permanent effect
for cryptocurrency returns since this effect is consistently positive and stronger for
weekly compared to daily returns. Non-linear ML models conditioning on order flow
outperform ML models conditioning on economic fundamentals as well as leading
ML benchmarks that do not include order flow. Forecast combinations of non-linear
ML models also exhibit strong performance. In short, order flow provides significant
predictive information that cannot be explained by economic fundamentals and non-
linear ML techniques best capture this information.

2.2 Data Overview
Our empirical analysis employs data from a cross-section of 84 cryptocurrencies for
the sample period of January 1, 2018 to June 30, 2022. The CMC database from
which we obtain the crypto prices contains data on thousands of coins but most
have low market capitalization and/or limited historical data. To obtain the final
cross-section of 84 coins, we impose three criteria to ensure the liquidity of our
sample. First, we require that coins must have a market capitalization of greater
than 1 million USD on the first day of the sample period. Second, coins must be
continuously traded with a non-zero price and non-zero volume in each time period
of our sample. Finally, third, we exclude all stable coins from the cross-section.
Order flow, is defined as the log difference between buyer-initiated and seller-initiated
transaction volume denominated in a particular fiat currency over a period of time.
The fiat currencies we employ include the following: US dollar (USD), Euro (EUR),
British pound (GBP), Japanese yen (JPY), Swiss frank (CHF), Canadian dollar
(CAD), Australian dollar (AUD), New Zealand dollar (NZD), Norwegian krone
(NOK), Swedish krona (SEK) and the Korean won (KR W). Following Menkhoff
42
et al. (2016), we standardize each order flow series using a rolling window of 30
days. Due to the standardization, the first day of our sample becomes February 14,
2018. World order flow is computed by aggregating all 11 buy-volumes and all 11
sell-volumes, taking the log difference, and performing the same standardization as
applied to the disaggregated order flows.
We use a combination of coin-specific variables and economic fundamentals as con-
trols in the empirical analysis. The coin-specific variables include market cap, total
volume, and volatility. The economic fundamentals used in our analysis include the
short rate, term spread, default spread, TED spread, S&P 500 index returns, MSCI
global index returns, and the VIX index returns. 3
2.3 Machine Learning Models
We perform an out-of-sample analysis of the predictive ability of order flow for cryp-
tocurrency returns using popular machine learning methods (see, e.g., Gu, Kelly and
Xiu, 2020). Out-of-sample forecasting of asset returns is notoriously difficult (see,
e.g., Welch and Goyal, 2008), which is likely exacerbated in the case of cryptos due to
their high volatility. Traditional prediction methods based on ordinary least squares
(OLS) regressions are likely to underperform when conditioning on a high number
of predictors such as the 11 international order flows and the additional control vari-
ables that we use to forecast one-day ahead cryptocurrency returns. ML methods

of predictors as well as a richer specification of functional forms.
We use linear regression estimated with pooled OLS as our benchmark model, which
is motivated by the predictive panel regression approach in the in-sample analysis of
the previous chapter. In addition to the OLS benchmark, we consider a variety of
linear and non-linear models from the rapidly expanding ML literature on forecasting
asset returns (Gu, Kelly and Xiu, 2020). We include Ridge regression (RR), Lasso
(LAS) and the Elastic Net (EN) for penalized linear models and principal component
regression (PCR) as a linear dimension reduction technique. Our non-linear mod-
els include tree-based models, random forest (RF) and stochastic gradient boosted
regression trees (SGB), and feed-forward neural networks with 1 to 4 hidden layers
(NN1-NN4). In what follows, we provide a detailed explanation of each model used
in our analysis as well as the objective function used in the estimation framework.

Principal component regression (PCR) is a dimension reduction technique that in-

transforms a set of predictors into orthogonal principal components, in which the first
component has the highest variance, the second component has the second highest
variance, and so on, up to K < Pprincipal components.

2.3.5 Random Forest and Gradient Boosted Regression Trees
Regression trees are fully nonparametric and follow a different logic from linear mod-

the final outputs are the average values of ri,t+1 within each region.

Trees capture complex non-linearities and multiway interactions among predictors,
however this flexibility is also a limitation that often leads trees to overfit on the
training sample. To address this issue, we use two types of ensemble models that
improve stability by aggregating over many trees.
The first type of ensemble model is random forest (RF), which is a special case
49
of a more general technique known as bagging. The standard bagging procedure
constructs B regression trees using B new bootstrap samples and averages their
predictions. These trees are usually deep and complex, hence each tree has low bias
but high variance. Averaging over all B trees reduces this variance, stabilizing the
model’s predictive performance.
RF uses a variation on bagging to further decrease the correlation among trees from
different bootstrap samples (Breiman, 2001). Unlike standard bagging, where all P
predictors are considered at each split, RF reduces tree correlation by only allowing
a random subset of m < Ppredictors to be considered for each split. This procedure
enhances the variance reduction of the model beyond what is achieved with standard
bagging, overall improving model stability.
The second type of ensemble model is stochastic gradient boosted regression trees
(SGB), which grows regression trees sequentially (Friedman, 2001). These trees are
typically shallow with few or often one split, hence each tree has low variance but
high bias. Trees are recursively built using the information from previous trees,
which reduces this bias by improving the model in areas where it does not perform
well.
The SGB algorithm begins by fitting the training data with a single shallow tree.
Next, a second shallow tree of the same depth is fitted based on the residuals from
the first tree. The prediction from these two trees are summed together to form
an ensemble prediction, but the second tree prediction is scaled by a learning rate
λ ∈ (0, 1) to help the model learn slowly and prevent overfitting. This procedure is
50
repeated until there are B trees in the ensemble, and the final prediction is the sum
of the scaled forecasts from each tree.
Furthermore, SGB uses a regularization technique where a random subsample of the
training data is used to fit each tree. This randomness reduces correlations between
trees, which improves model stability and computation speed.
For both types of ensemble models, we tune the number of trees,B, and the maximum
depth, L, using the validation set. For SGB, we also tune the learning rate λ. See
Section 2.4 for a detailed discussion.

train the neural network by using the stochastic gradient descent (SGD) algorithm,
which jointly updates all parameters at each iteration using the gradient calculated
from a random subsample of the training data. SGD is somewhat less accurate
at each step compared to the traditional gradient descent, however it significantly
improves computation speed. To further enhance computational efficiency, we use the
Adaptive Moment Estimation (Adam) algorithm to adaptively control the learning
rate, reducing it when the gradient is close to zero.
Estimating neural networks necessitates substantial regularization due to their heavy
parameterization and severe non-linearities, hence we apply several techniques in-
spired by Gu, Kelly and Xiu (2020). First, we include an ℓ1 penalty into the loss
function, which functions similarly to the lasso, encouraging a sparse representation
of the predictors. Second, we use early stopping to mitigate overfitting, by termi-
nating the estimation procedure when the validation error begins to increase. Early
stopping acts as a less computationally intensive alternative to ℓ2 penalization, while
still providing shrinkage. Third, we use batch normalization to control the variability
of predictors across different regions in the network. Fourth, we adopt an ensemble
approach by training ten different neural networks initialized with different seeds,
and averaging their forecasts. Since each seed produces a different forecast, this en-
semble approach reduces forecast variance. Section 2.4 provides further details on

the hyperparameters used in these techniques.

perform well even when the market portfolio exhibits a negative return in the last
year of the sample.
Overall, we can summarize the main results as follows: (1) the non-linear ML OF
models perform the best and their performance is excellent by any standard. For
example, the long-short portfolio for the best model (SGB) delivers a daily mean
return of 0.76% and an annualized SR = 3.68; (2) the linear ML models consistently
underperform the non-linear models, demonstrating that model complexity is impor-
tant for generating economic value; (3) the OLS benchmark performs well relative to
regularized linear models; (4) the combined forecasts are among the best performers;
(5) the portfolios sorted on the daily ML models perform substantially better than
portfolios sorted directly on world order flow; and, finally, (6) ML models condition-
ing on OF outperform leading benchmarks that use ML models without conditioning
on OF. In short, there is high economic value in conditioning on all 11 international

order flows in the context of non-linear ML models.

The combination of non-linear machine learning techniques with international order

3.1 Introduction
The previous two chapters have established strong evidence of the explanatory and
predictive power of order flow for the cross-section of cryptocurrency returns. In the
first chapter we used in-sample panel regressions at the daily and weekly levels and
establish strong evidence for the significance of world order flow in both explaining
and predicting returns. Our results show the predictive information contained in or-
der flow is permanent rather than transitory in nature. Chapter two employed linear
and non-linear ML models in addition to forecast combinations to predict returns
in an out-of-sample setting. The out-performance of non-linear ML techniques for
models conditioning on all international order flows and world order flow suggests
these variables contain complex and potentially non-linear information about future
75
returns.
In this chapter, we take our analysis one step further to shed light on important
questions related to the non-linear nature of the information contained in order flow,
and the profitability of our trading strategy under economic restrictions and limits
to arbitrage. Addressing these topics will deepen our understanding of the reason
behind the strong performance of non-linear machine learning models that condition
on order flow, in addition to building upon the economic insight to our previous
results from the trading strategies.
Specifically, we address the following four questions. First, why do non-linear ma-
chine learning models that condition on order flow improve the predictability of
returns? Second, is there a bidirectional relationship between order flow and re-
turns? Third, are the portfolio results robust to short-sale constraints, drawdowns
and transaction costs? Fourth, can the economic value of order flow be explained by
limits to arbitrage?
Our empirical analysis employs the same daily data used in the previous two chap-
ters. This dataset consists of a cross-section of 84 cryptocurrencies for the sample
period of January 1, 2018 to June 30, 2022. It includes a rich dataset of order flows
denominated in 11 fiat currencies, including the G10 currencies plus South Korea in
addition to an aggregate world order flow. In what follows, we discuss our empirical
approach and main findings step by step.
We begin our analysis by identifying the existence of significant non-linear relation-
ships between order flow and returns. By estimating a panel regression conditioning
76
on non-linear transformations of the international and world order flows, we show
clear evidence of a non-linear relationship between returns and order flow. This
provides measurable evidence to explain why non-linear ML techniques outperform
linear ones when it comes to models conditioning on order flow. Next, we test for
the existence of a bidirectional relationship between order flow and returns. Follow-
ing Foucault, Pagano and Roell (2013), we estimate a vector autoregression (V AR)
model with the endogenous variables being returns and order flows. We find mixed
evidence in support of a bidirectional relationship when we estimate individual V AR
models for the top 10 coins by market cap. Out of these 10 coins, about half show
significance of past returns on future order flow suggesting that the existence of
mechanical price pressure is coin-specific. This relationship becomes stronger when
estimated over the cross-section of the top-10 coins as well as for all 84 coins.
Motivated by Avramov, Cheng and Metzker (2023), we conduct an analysis on the
profitability of our models in the presence of economic restrictions such as short-
selling constraints, transaction costs, drawdowns and limits to arbitrage. To be
more specific, in the cryptocurrency market, it may be difficult or even impossible
to short certain coins. Therefore, to generate a realistic trading strategy that avoids
shorting, we may focus on the long portfolio rather than the long-short portfolio
spread. In light of this, we show that compared to the long-short portfolios, the long
portfolios exhibit a similar risk-adjusted return, lower Sharpe ratio, lower turnover
and hence a higher break-even transaction cost. For example, the long portfolio of the
NL-Mean combination exhibits a daily alpha of 0.87% (t-stat=3.18), an annualized
Sharpe ratio of 1.98 and a break-even transaction cost of 1.07% per day. In contrast,
77
the same model for the long-short portfolio delivers an alpha of 0.81% (t-stat=5.83),
an annualized Sharpe ratio of 3.61 and a break-even transaction cost of 0.50% per
day. We conclude, therefore, that ML models that condition on order flow generate
high economic value for long-only investors who pursue a realistic trading strategy
investing in cryptocurrencies.
Finally, we study whether the profitability of ML models conditioning on order flow
can be explained by limits to arbitrage. We address this in two ways. First, we
restrict our analysis to the top 10 coins by market capitalization which tend to have
low arbitrage costs. If limits to arbitrage were present, we would expect to see
the profitability diminish when restricted to the largest of coins in our sample. We
find the non-linear ML models conditioning on order flow continue to perform well
and in some cases perform better than models that use the full cross-section. For
example, the best performing model using the full cross-section is SGB OF with an
R2
oos = 0.39% which almost triples to 1.05% with the top 10 coins. Additionally, the
profitability of the ML models remains strong. For example, the SGB OF remains
the best performing model with a Sharpe ratio of 2.14.
Second, following Atilgan et al. (2020) and Liu, Tsyvinski and Wu (2022) we create
an arbitrage cost index based on multiple indicators known to capture limits to arbi-
trage. We find that orthogonalized order flow and non-linear ML models conditioning
on order flow produce significant economic value for coins with low arbitrage costs. In
contrast, models conditioning on economic fundamentals perform well only for coins
with higher arbitrage costs, indicating that their economic value can be explained by
limits to arbitrage. Overall, the inability of limits to arbitrage to explain the positive
78
predictive ability of order flow, especially in the context of non-linear ML models,
further supports the permanent view of the effect of order flow on cryptocurrency
returns.
In summary, our analysis provides compelling empirical evidence to establish that
order flow has strong and economically valuable out-of-sample predictive power for
cryptocurrency returns. Our empirical findings are robust to economic restrictions,
such as short-selling constraints and high transaction costs that characterize the
cryptocurrency market.
In general, our approach is distinct from the existent literature as indicated by the
use of a large cross-section of coins, a large cross-section of order flows denominated
in different fiat currencies, the out-of-sample focus of assessing predictability, the use
of machine learning methods and the economic evaluation based on portfolio sorts
with an assessment of the effect of limits to arbitrage.
The remainder of the paper is organized as follows. In the next section, we review the
cryptocurrency dataset used in the empirical analysis. In Section 3, we present our
panel results highlighting the non-linear relationship between order flow and returns
and discuss the results from the V AR analysis. In Section 4, we assess the economic
value of order flow based on portfolio sorts in presence of economic restrictions and
discuss the impact limits to arbitrage have on our overall profitability. Finally, we
conclude in Section 5.

3.2 Data Overview
Our empirical analysis employs data from a cross-section of 84 cryptocurrencies for
the sample period of January 1, 2018 to June 30, 2022. The CMC database from
which we obtain the crypto prices contains data on thousands of coins but most
have low market capitalization and/or limited historical data. To obtain the final
cross-section of 84 coins, we impose three criteria to ensure the liquidity of our
sample. First, we require that coins must have a market capitalization of greater
than 1 million USD on the first day of the sample period. Second, coins must be
continuously traded with a non-zero price and non-zero volume in each time period
of our sample. Finally, third, we exclude all stable coins from the cross-section.
Order flow, is defined as the log difference between buyer-initiated and seller-initiated
transaction volume denominated in a particular fiat currency over a period of time.
The fiat currencies we employ include the following: US dollar (USD), Euro (EUR),
British pound (GBP), Japanese yen (JPY), Swiss frank (CHF), Canadian dollar
(CAD), Australian dollar (AUD), New Zealand dollar (NZD), Norwegian krone
(NOK), Swedish krona (SEK) and the Korean won (KR W). Following Menkhoff
et al. (2016), we standardize each order flow series using a rolling window of 30
days. Due to the standardization, the first day of our sample becomes February 14,
2018. World order flow is computed by aggregating all 11 buy-volumes and all 11
sell-volumes, taking the log difference, and performing the same standardization as
applied to the disaggregated order flows.
We use a combination of coin-specific variables and economic fundamentals as con-
80
trols in the empirical analysis. The coin-specific variables include market cap, total
volume, and volatility. The economic fundamentals used in our analysis include the
short rate, term spread, default spread, TED spread, S&P 500 index returns, MSCI
global index returns, and the VIX index returns. 1
3.3 Nature of the Relationship Between Order Flow
and Returns
3.3.1 Non-linear Prediction
Through the work in the first two chapters, we have shown the significance of order
flow for predicting cryptocurrency returns both in and out of sample. In chapter 2
we found evidence that order flow has strong out-of-sample predictability as linear
and non-linear ML models conditioning on order flow perform well, with non-liner
models performing the best. One of the limitations of ML techniques such as the
ones used in the previous chapter is the ”black box” nature of the models. It is
not obvious as to why the non-linear models perform better than the linear order
flow models. Could it be the case that order flow has a non-linear relationship to
future returns? We shed light on this question by specifying a single predictive panel
regression, where world and international order flows enter in a linear, square and
cubic form together with interaction terms between the world order flow and each

3.3.2 V AR models
Although we have established order flow has strong predictive power for returns, it
is well documented in the literature that prices and order flows affect each other in a
dynamic way (see, e.g., Foucault, Pagano and Roell, 2013). The positive correlation
between prices and order flows may stem from two factors. First, informed traders
buy an asset, when they believe that the price will go up. In this case, order flow
contains predictive information. Second, it is possible that high order flow also
creates mechanical price pressure, which can be observed in two directions. In the
presence of momentum, a higher price will attract higher order flow, which in turn
83
will push up the price leading to a feedback effect. Alternatively, in the presence
of contrarian reversal and inventory effects, a lower price may attract buy market
orders and a higher price may attract sell market orders leading to an effect that is the
opposite of momentum. The direction of price pressure can be assessed empirically.
While the correlation between order flow and future returns establishes the predictive
power of order flow, we gain greater economic insight by evaluating the relative
role of the two causal effects. To disentangle the two effects, we estimate a vector
autoregression (V AR) in which the endogenous variables are daily cryptocurrency
returns and order flows (up to five lags), and the exogenous variables are the same
control variables that we use in the panel regressions in chapter 1. This is similar
to testing for Granger causality of returns on order flows and order flows on returns.
Following Hasbrouck (1991), we include contemporaneous world order flow in the
V AR regressions. We estimate V AR regressions for each of the top 10 coins by
market cap as well as for all top-10 coins together and all 84 coins together (i.e., the
full cross section). In Table 3.3, we report results for select coefficients of all V AR
regressions.3

highly significant for returns in all coins. For example, the t-statistic on BTC is
equal to 12.05. Second, in the presence of contemporaneous world order flow, the
predictive world order flow is significant for the returns of half of the top 10 coins.
For example, t-statistic for XRP is equal to 2.20. More importantly, third, when

3.4 Economic Restrictions and Limits to Arbitrage
3.4.1 Short-sale Constraints, Drawdowns, and Transaction
Costs
Our portfolio analysis has so far focused on the long-short portfolio because it is
well suited for illustrating the economic value of conditioning on order flow. In other
words, if order flow is a good predictor, it should be able to identify which portfolio
performs well and which one does not. Then, investors would benefit from a zero-cost
trading strategy that goes long on the top portfolio and short on the bottom portfolio.
However, in the cryptocurrency market, it may be difficult, even impossible, to short
some cryptocurrencies. When possible, additional shorting fees will apply (see, e.g.,
Liu, Tsyvinski and Wu, 2022). Since the objective of a typical investor is to generate
a realistic trading strategy that avoids shorting and is subject to lower transaction
costs, in this section we focus on just the long portfolio. In Table 3.5, we report
detailed results on the performance of the daily long OF portfolio ( P5) and compare
them to the corresponding daily long-short OF portfolio ( P5 − P1).
For a comprehensive assessment of performance of the most realistic trading strate-
gies, we report the mean return, alpha, beta on the market portfolio, and Sharpe
ratio. Additionally, in assessing economic restrictions, we report the maximum draw-

3.4.2 Limits to Arbitrage
Our main result is that order flow itself as well as ML models that condition on order
flow positively predict future crypto returns. It might be expected that arbitragers
would identify this opportunity and drive prices toward their fundamental values.
However, Shleifer and Vishny (1997) and Pontiff (2006) argue that there are limits to
arbitrage, which in our context could potentially explain the strong positive relation
between order flow and future returns. A testable implication of this argument is
that the relation between order flow and future returns should be more pronounced
in coins that are more difficult to arbitrage.
Rather than relying on a single proxy for arbitrage costs, we follow Atilgan et al.
(2020) and Liu, Tsyvinski and Wu (2022) in constructing an arbitrage index (AI)
using a number of indicators known to capture important dimensions of limits to
arbitrage. First, we build an index at the coin level, for which we include size,
idiosyncratic volatility (Ang et al., 2006), and illiquidity (Amihud, 2002). 5 To con-
struct the AI, we sort coins in increasing order based on their idiosyncratic volatility
and illiquidity, and decreasing order based on their size. Each coin is given the cor-
5 Idiosyncratic volatility is defined as the volatility of residuals from the three factor model (Liu,
Tsyvinski and Wu, 2022) over the previous 21 days. Illiquidity is defined as the ratio of the
absolute value of returns over daily total volume (Amihud, 2002), averaged over the past 21
days.
89
responding score of its quintile rank for each variable. Then, the arbitrage cost index
is the sum of the three scores, which ranges from 3 to 15. A higher value implies
greater limits to arbitrage.

We also conduct a bivariate sort analysis. First, at time t, we split the coins in our

These results demonstrate that the economic value of order flow cannot be explained
by limits to arbitrage, as the long-short performance is strongest in coins, which are
the easiest to arbitrage, i.e., the largest, most liquid, and lowest volatility coins.
90
The second panel shows that for the low and medium AI terciles, the long-short

coins with the highest arbitrage costs.
Importantly, the third panel reveals that for NL-Mean, all AI terciles display sig-

are remarkable considering that the economic value of ML models not conditioning
on order flow can mostly be explained by limits to arbitrage in stocks (Avramov,
Cheng and Metzker, 2023), options (Bali et al., 2023), and cryptos (Cakici et al.,
2024, and Filippou, Rapach and Thimsen, 2024).
Table 3.7 presents a bivariate sort analysis for L-Mean and NL-Mean conditioning
purely on economic fundamentals (EF). For both models, the Sharpe ratios are the
lowest in the bottom AI tercile and increase monotonically as we move to the top
AI tercile. This clearly indicates that the long-short portfolio returns for models
conditioning on economic fundamentals have economic value only for small, illiquid,
and volatile coins.
Overall, these results show that the economic value of orthogonalized order flow
and non-linear ML models cannot be explained by limits to arbitrage. In contrast,
arbitrage costs can explain the economic value of linear ML models conditioning on
91
order flow as well as (linear and non-linear) ML models conditioning on economic
fundamentals. In light of these results, we conclude that the permanent effect of
order flow on crypto returns is not limited to small, illiquid and volatile coins but
rather extends to coins which are large, liquid and less volatile.

3.5 Conclusion
The meteoric rise of cryptocurrencies as a new investment asset class has triggered an
emerging literature in financial economics. A central theme of this literature is the
out-of-sample prediction of cryptocurrency returns and the design of trading strate-
gies that demonstrate consistent profitability. Our analysis builds on the results
established in the second chapter, deepening our understanding of the economic
value of order flow in predicting cryptocurrency returns over and above economic
fundamentals. We provide measurable evidence of the existence of a non-linear re-
lationship between order flow and returns, which explains why the non-linear ML
models conditioning on order flow out-perform linear ones. We also show that a
bidirectional relationship between returns and order flow is coin specific and not all
coins are driven by mechanical price pressure. However, the relationship strengthens
when estimating over the cross-section of the top-10 and all 84 coins.
We further expand upon our understanding of the profitability of the trading strate-
gies developed in the previous chapter. The profitability of the non-linear order flow
models are robust to economic restrictions such as short selling constraints, trans-
action costs, and limits to arbitrage, providing compelling empirical support for the
permanent view of the effect of order flow on returns. Our bivariate sort analysis
shows that non-linear order flow models produce profitable results that are not re-
stricted to the small, illiquid, and highly volatile coins. This is further supported
by the positive economic value seen by restricting our analysis to the top 10 coins
by market cap. This evidence is remarkable considering that the economic value of
94
ML models that do not condition on order flow can largely be explained by lim-
its to arbitrage in the stock market, options, and crypto literature. In conclusion,
our empirical evidence strongly indicates that information conveyed by order flow is
not only important for predicting cryptocurrency returns, but is robust to economic
restrictions as well.
